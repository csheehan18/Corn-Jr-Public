Invent the world, and the copy discipline generalises

The twelfth finding diagnosed tool.copy 0/120 as a corpus defect rather than a model result: web_search's answer was a function of its query over 28 hand-written facts, 107 training examples each, so nothing ever required reading <|obs|>. That is a falsifiable claim, and the fix is the test of it. corn/tools.py now invents the search index — ~8,700 facts over twelve families — and data/raw/tools.txt is regenerated at the same 10,000 examples, so the answer space is the only thing that moved.

120/120, with an empty failure table. temporal.copy and tool.copy are both at ceiling on the same checkpoint, which is what "the discipline generalises" has to mean, and the "still one tool" caveat carried since the sixth finding is discharged. Nothing about the model changed — the language-modelling rows are flat to the fourth decimal (0.3666 → 0.3665). What changed is that the corpus stopped answering the question for him.

trained_answer is now zero everywhere, which is the mechanism confirmed from the other side. The upsampled arm's 22 and 7 remaining failures are ordinary wrong_answer: there is no memorisable table left to recite, so the specific pathology the twelfth finding caught — William Shakespeare when the tool had said Alexander Fleming — has no way to occur. The eleventh finding's send_text collapse also improved, missing:message 16 → 5.

Repetition still costs something, and it is no longer the same something

The one-pass arm is perfect; the 15% arm, reading the same corpus 24 times, is the only arm that fails the row at all. So upsampling is still not free, but the old explanation is gone with the old table. Whether the residue is repetition or capacity is the confound the eleventh finding named and this run does not break — a bigger distinct slice at a fixed 15% is the experiment, and it needs the sentences §11 still has no source for.

Three bugs, all caught by guards rather than by the training run

Writing the checks before the corpus is what made the fix survivable. None of these would have raised an error; each would have produced a number.

  • An answer equal to its own query. Pools were drawn independently from one name space, so the same string landed in two of them: the capital of Belaburg -> Belaburg. That restores the exact property the rebuild exists to remove. Fixed by carving disjoint pools from one prefix-free master list.
  • A generated answer colliding with the holdout. Lake depths draw from [120, 1990] and HELD_OUT_FACTS says Baikal is 1,642 metres at its deepest — which the generator produced on its first run. A model could then learn that string in training and pass the Baikal item without reading anything, a false positive on the one row built to detect exactly that.
  • Pattern prefixes. web_search_tool matches with p in q or q in p, so the capital of Bela swallows the capital of Belaria and the tool answers by table order rather than by query.

An audit of the old tables came back clean on all three axes, which is worth recording: the twelfth finding really was about the size of the answer space and not a lookup bug. check_tools.py now asserts all of it, including the exposures-per-fact ratio itself — if a later edit pushes 1.6 back toward 107, the guard fails rather than the finding quietly reversing.

A fourth defect was subtler and is the one worth remembering. The group-12 checks pinned the word-boundary rule with the literals "It's Paris." and "It's 6.". When FACTS became generated those strings stopped being training answers, and the checks failed — correctly, but the lesson is the near miss: a check that hardcodes its fixtures silently stops testing what it names. Had those literals happened to stay valid, the checks would have kept passing while asserting nothing. They now draw from the live table, longest answer for the recitation case and shortest for the boundary case, since the shortest is the one most able to appear inside another string by accident.

The selection rule lost its first real test, inside its own noise

This is the first pair where ckpt.pt and last.pt are different files, so §11's standing question — how often do the rule and the ruler disagree — finally has an answer. The rule picked iter 5,125 in both arms; the blended val preferred 5,237; the frozen set prefers 5,237 on all three lm rows in the one-pass arm and two of three in the upsampled one. The blended val agreed with the ruler and the stratified rule did not, which is the reverse of the seventh finding that motivated the rule.

The margins are the whole story. The rule preferred 5,125 by 0.0079 and 0.0009 — 0.79% and 0.09% relative, against its own measured relative noise of 0.94%. Both disagreements happened inside the noise floor, so the rule is not choosing badly; it is choosing at a resolution it does not have. And val cannot see what actually moved: tool.selection went 148 → 116 across those same 112 iterations, chose_resolve_date 19 → 56, while every per-source loss stayed flat to three decimals. Behaviour is not a smooth function of validation loss at the tail of a cosine schedule, and no val-based rule will fix that.

What this does not settle: one pair at one scale, and tool.selection's 32-point swing between adjacent checkpoints is itself a warning that some of these rows are noisier than the differences being read off them. The 0.61% arm's selection number should be treated as a band, not a point.
