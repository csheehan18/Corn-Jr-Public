Tool calls and copying · 17 August 2026

Copying fails to generalise because the training facts were memorisable

The result, as the finding states it

The copy discipline does not generalise, and the corpus is why

Superseded by the thirteenth finding, which acted on this one's diagnosis and reversed its headline. Everything below stands as measured; what it could not know is that the cause it identifies is sufficient. Rebuilding the search index took tool.copy from 27/120 to 120/120 with no change to the model. Read this as the diagnosis and the thirteenth as the confirmation — "the copy discipline does not generalise" was true of this corpus and is not true of the tool.

temporal.copy is the row every conclusion in this project rests on. It reads 353/353 in all three arms of the eleventh finding, as it has through templates, real web text, retokenization, and four rival tools. DATA.md §11 has carried "still one tool" as the standing caveat on it since the sixth finding, and the tenth finding shaped web_search to return an answer field precisely so the same behaviour could be asked of a second tool. tool.copy is that row: cut after <|obs|>, score the <|say|>, answers drawn from HELD_OUT_FACTS so a correct one proves copying rather than knowing.

The same checkpoint that defers to resolve_date 353 times out of 353 defers to web_search 27 times out of 120 at best, and zero at worst. The behaviour §4's whole conclusion describes is not a disposition the model has. It is a thing it does with one tool.

The 0% arm's failure is the cleanest statement of the floor: asked what to say after a web_search observation, it emits <|call|>{"name": "recall_day", ...} — the continuation the temporal format taught it — 120 times out of 120. It has the script and applies it to the wrong tool.

The upsampled arm recites the training fact table

The 15% arm scores zero, and 52 of its 120 failures name a training answer outright:

want 'Alexander Fleming' got <|say|>It's William Shakespeare. want '3,422 degrees Celsius' got <|say|>It's Mount Everest, at 8,849 metres. want 'up to 3.5 metres' got <|say|>Looks like eleven a side on the pitch.

Every one of those is a FACTS entry, read straight out of the weights with the tool's answer sitting in the prompt above it. This is the sixth finding's year-clamping again — a model substituting the training distribution's value for the one it was handed — but total rather than confined to one field, and it is the same memorisation that broke send_text in the eleventh finding, showing up on a second row.

The direction is what makes it damning. The one-pass arm gets Kinshasa 20/20; the upsampled arm gets it 0/20. More tool data, twenty-five times over, took a behaviour from working to absent.

The corpus cannot teach deference on this tool, and the arithmetic says so

The scored row is strict — it wants the tool's answer string, which is what the corpus always writes. A softer reading, asking only whether the answer's distinctive token survived at all:

Both readings agree, and the per-answer breakdown in the one-pass arm points at the cause: Kinshasa 20/20, Alexander Fleming 7/20, and 0 for the three multi-word measurement answers — up to 3.5 metres comes back as about 3.5 metres, 1,642 metres at its deepest as 1,642 metres.. Partial copies, regenerated rather than transcribed.

Then the arithmetic. data/raw/tools.txt holds 3,000 search transcripts over 28 facts — 107 examples per fact, and 2,679 in the upsampled arm. web_search's answer is a deterministic function of its query, and there are twenty-eight of them. A model can score 100% on the training distribution of this tool without ever reading <|obs|>, so nothing in the corpus ever requires it to.

resolve_date is not like that, and not by luck. Its answer depends on the <|day|> anchor as well as the utterance, and the anchors span 2020–2027 — so "last Tuesday" has no fixed answer and the observation is the only place the date can come from. The temporal slice makes deference load-bearing by construction. The tool slice made it optional, and the model took the option.

So this is a corpus defect, not a model result, which is the useful form of the finding: it predicts its own fix. Widen the answer space until the query stops determining the answer — more facts than a model can memorise at the exposure the mix implies, or answers generated per example rather than drawn from a table. Until then tool.validity and tool.selection are measuring something the slice does teach, and tool.copy is measuring something it does not.

What this costs the sixth finding, and what it does not

The sixth finding's ordering stands: pretrained 100% against 48–92% across six fine-tuned checkpoints, on the same row, is unaffected by anything here. What narrows is the claim. "Deferring to a tool over your own prior is the part that has to be there from the start" should now read: deferring to resolve_date is. Whether that generalises is exactly what this row was built to test, and the answer on the only second tool available is no — for a reason that is about how the second tool's data was generated rather than about deference.

DATA.md §11 already recorded the analogous limit on temporal.copy itself — that its power comes from the anchor year being out of distribution. This is the same lesson arriving from the other side: a copy row measures copying only where the answer could not have come from anywhere else, and that is a property of the training corpus, not of the eval.

What this does not settle: 25M parameters, and the one-pass arm is the only one that shows the behaviour at all, at 22.5%. Whether a widened fact table gets web_search to resolve_date's 100%, or whether something about dates makes them uniquely copyable, needs the corpus fix and a re-run — which is now a generation change and two training runs, not an argument.
