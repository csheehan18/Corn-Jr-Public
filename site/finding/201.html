Understanding at small scale · 22 October 2026

Words picked at random gain less than words the scheduler picked

The result, as the finding states it

With the scheduler's words drawn at random — as many of each frequency band as the surprise arm steered, under the same cap, spacing and variety — the held-out rare-word gap reads 7.814 and 7.923 against the surprise arm's 7.650 and 7.802 and the base's 7.896 and 8.064: the pre-registered between row (m), random choice helps less and not separably at the gate. The steered-target split separates them: rare targets (10-30 sightings) chosen at random fall 0.70 and 0.13 nats in their own held-out sentences against 1.75 and 0.98 for those the surprise scheduler chose, though the random ones got more rows each (4.2 against 2.7-3.2). The progress score is noise (finding 200), so the difference lies in what comes with it: the surprise scheduler steers a word only once he has met it three times and still finds it harder than average (z +1.2 at the first steer, random picks +0.1), which lands its rare-word steering mid-run (median first steer at step 2,600-3,100 against 900-1,100). Fluency, chats and the understanding reads hold at both seeds

The order holds at both seeds and in both bands, and it does not come from dose. The random rare words got more rows than the surprise ones and fell less. Nor from picking harder words: the surprise seeds' rare targets were harder than their band at seed 1 (19.56 nats against 18.05) and easier at seed 2 (17.38) and fell most at both. The groups are small (20-29 words, the per-word drops spread several nats), so the size of the gap between arms is a direction; its sign is the same four times.

Why a noise score still chooses better than random

Finding 200: the progress score is noise — a frozen model picks the same words. But the score is not the whole rule. A word is eligible only once he has met it three times in his own reading and while he still finds it harder than the average candidate (fast > 0), and only then does the noise rank it. That gate has two effects the random control removed:

  • the word is one he has started on and not finished — at the first steer its recent surprise sits 1.2-1.3 above the average candidate's; the random picks sit at the average;
  • the steering lands when the model can use it — a rare word reaches three sightings only well into the run, so the surprise arm's rare-word steering starts near step 2,600-3,100 and ends by 3,500-3,900; the random arm starts within the first thousand steps, during warmup and the model's first pass at language, and spreads to the end.

Which of the two does the work is not separable here.

What it licenses

  • Keep the gate, drop the progress score. The part of the scheduler that beats random is "met a few times, still hard"; the ranking by learning progress adds nothing a frozen model does not (finding 200). The arm that tests it: steer eligible words (3+ sightings, fast > 0) in random order, or ranked by fast — how hard the word still is — against the surprise arm as it stands.
  • Timing needs its own control: the same random set with steering held back until a fixed step (say 2,000) separates "started on" from "late enough to keep".
  • Nothing here reaches understanding at 5M — fields, wishes and lift stay inside the base pair's spread in every arm (random seed 1's wishes 0.38 is one seed, not a gate) — so the 20M is still where the reading change has to show it, with the gate-only rule beside the full one.

What it does not settle. Which half of the gate does the work; any size above 5M; whether the per-word order survives larger target groups (the held-out read has 100 words a band, of which an arm steers 20-46).
