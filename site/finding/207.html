The reply judge · 22 October 2026

The pairs he fails are mostly decidable: he loses the conversation's thread

The result, as the finding states it

Matching blind — two messages from one conversation, their two replies shuffled, the answer key closed — a person decides 42 of 50 same-dialogue quads the plain 5M (seed 1) gets wrong, and 38 of those correctly; on 25 random quads (which the model mostly gets right) 23 decided, 23 correct. So three quarters of the model's same-dialogue failures (38 of 50) are pairs a reader can settle, and only 8 have nothing that decides them — the opposite of finding 206's hand read of six quads, which is corrected here. What decides the quads the model fails is mostly the conversation's order (20 of 38: which reply sets up which message — "Why did you choose Max as your new name?" is what "I don't really know, it just felt right" answers), then a picked-up detail (10), then a direct question answered (7); what decides the ones it gets right is mostly a direct question answered (16 of 23)

What it cost

scripts/probe_quad_handcount.py, the draw (2,000 quads on both control seeds, 1 min of card, contended) and the blind read of 75 quads, 14:15-14:35 on 2026-10-22. ETA 20 min, actual ~20.

The question (finding 206's licence, the owner: "hand count, really see what you can learn"). The matching exercise did not take at a tenth of the conversational reading, and six hand-read quads suggested much of the control's error has no right answer. If so, a heavier weight would train on noise. This counts it properly: blind, with a baseline.

The draw. 2,000 same-dialogue quads (seed 7, used by no training run); the seed-1 control gets 286 wrong (14.3%). Fifty of those and 25 random quads, shuffled together; per quad the two messages under A and B and the two replies under 1 and 2 in a coin-flipped order. The reader writes which reply answers A (or ?) and what decided it, before the key is opened (evals/results/quadcount__*).

Read by hand

Conversation order is the large class: a reply that the other message goes on to answer or follow. A: "I used them this morning ... can't remember where." B: "I checked my room and they're not there." — "Okay, so we know you had them this morning. Where did you go after you left your room?" answers A, and "When was the last time you remember using them?" is what A itself answers. A: "Okay, bye." B: "Aww, I missed you too. I'm almost home." — "Bye." and "Great! I can't wait to see you." Each needs the turn's place in the exchange, not just its topic. The model handles the question-and-answer kind (the random quads it gets right are mostly that) and loses the thread.

The undecidable eight are real: two "you're welcome" turns against two thank-yous, two agreeing turns in a debate, two congratulations on a win — the pattern finding 206 saw, at a sixth of the failures, not most of them.

Four calls were wrong — order cases where both sequences read plausibly; a person's ceiling on these quads is below 100%.

What it licenses

  • The exercise has headroom: about three quarters of the control's same-dialogue misses are decidable, so a heavier weight trains mostly on signal, not noise. Finding 206's "(u) at this weight" stands; its reason does not.
  • What to aim it at is the thread of a conversation, not its topic: the failures are about where a turn sits, which is the owner's coherence target in miniature — answering this message, not the conversation.
  • Next by this count: the exercise at a heavier weight, same-dialogue quads only, read by the same take read, the swap test beside, and the blind reply read.

What it does not settle. One reader, one seed's failures; whether a lesson on the thread carries into writing.
