Understanding at small scale · 20 October 2026

His surprise spots unusual words, not words he misunderstands

The result, as the finding states it

On the shipped 260M, the summed surprise of each word of a request picks out the one hard word or phrase in it 28 of 40 times (single words 25 of 32, idioms 3 of 8), separates hard words from ordinary content words at AUC 0.873, and at the threshold that flags 75% of the hard words fires on 0 of 16 plain requests and on every name (8 of 8) and typo (8 of 8); it is no higher where he gets the meaning wrong (AUC 0.530), and a plain count of how often each word appears in his web text does better (33 of 40, AUC 0.918)

What it cost

writing scripts/probe_surprise.py and its 72 requests 20:50-20:52 on 2026-10-20 (7ceb20b); the read on three checkpoints 28.9 s and twelve hand decodes 7.3 s, both contended (the card at 38-39%, a bound).

The question (owner, 2026-10-20). The design under discussion for 1.x: when a request carries a word he does not know, he asks the person once ("what do you mean by spendy?"), the answer goes in a per-instance glossary, and the runtime shows it beside the word every later time, so nobody answers the same question twice. The runtime cannot judge understanding. Can his own surprise — the probability he gives each word of the person's message, free from the prefill — say which word to ask about? "Let's do the surprise check first to get a sense of when he doesn't know."

The probe (registered at 7ceb20b with its prediction). Hand-written, nothing from any log: 40 requests each carrying one hard word or phrase — 12 slang, 9 regional, 11 rare, 8 idioms — with a 4-way meaning check; 16 plain requests; 8 with a name or place; 8 with a typo. Per word, the summed NLL of its tokens after <|user|>; a phrase is one unit at its most surprising word; the first word of a message is left out.

Against the prediction: single words 0.78 against 0.55-0.75, idioms 0.38 against 0.10-0.30 (both above); AUC inside 0.80-0.90; plain requests 0 against 10-25% (better); names and typos all fire (held); meaning AUC 0.53 against 0.55-0.65 — no relation at all; frequency at least as good (held, and more).

What it finds and what it misses. It finds the word that looks unusual: bougie 33.0 nats, knackered 34.2, brekkie 41.2, FOMO 27.5. It misses the hard word that looks ordinary — lit 8.1, sus 8.2, grub 14.9 (below late), ubiquitous 7.1 (below e-scooters) — and the idiom made of common words: break the bank 3.7, hit the hay, a stone's throw. The 20M flags every idiom, where the 260M flags three of eight.

Surprise is not understanding. The meaning check says he picks the right meaning of 18 of the 40; surprise is no higher on the 22 he misses. And the check flatters him: twelve free decodes ('Q: In "...", what does "X" mean? A: It means') answer none of them correctly — spendy "that you are willing to spend money on", dodgy "that the brakes feel heavy", on the cards "that the card is for tomorrow", and the rest echo the word or the request back. Of these 40, he understands close to none, so what surprise separates here is unusual against ordinary, not known against unknown.

What it licenses

  • A trigger, not a judge. Surprise says "this word is unusual", never fires on a plain request here, and is free: good enough to decide when to ask, at one question a message.
  • It needs two filters before it can ask. Every name and every typo fires. A name is not a question to ask (the people store and capitalisation catch most); a typo is a different question ("did you mean restaurant?").
  • It cannot catch the dangerous misses: a slang word spelled like a common one (lit, sus) or an idiom of common words. Those need the other two routes — the person's correction saved to the same glossary, or meanings shown where they matter.
  • The cheaper detector is at least as good. A word-count list of his own training text reads 33 of 40 at AUC 0.918 with no forward pass, so the build should read both and price them on real requests.

What it does not settle. Requests written by people other than the one who wrote these 72; a threshold chosen on real traffic; whether the two detectors combined beat either; any read of what he does with a glossary line once it is on the page (findings 186-187 say a described word is not yet read at 20M).
