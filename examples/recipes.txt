# The Weeknight Book

A short book of things worth cooking on a Tuesday. Written for this repo as a
document to hand Corn-Jr: every recipe is titled, every step is numbered, and
nothing in here needs a machine that costs more than the dinner.


# Mushroom Risotto

Serves four. It wants stirring and it wants your attention, and in exchange it
does not want anything expensive.

- 300g arborio rice
- 250g chestnut mushrooms, sliced thick
- 1 litre hot vegetable stock
- 1 onion, diced fine
- 50g butter
- a handful of grated parmesan

1. Warm the stock in a small pan and keep it at a bare simmer.
2. Fry the mushrooms in half the butter over a high heat until they squeak and
   colour, then lift them out and set them aside.
3. Drop the heat, add the rest of the butter, and soften the onion for about
   eight minutes without letting it brown.
4. Stir in the rice and toast it for two minutes, until the grains look glassy
   at the edges.
5. Add the stock one ladle at a time, stirring, waiting for each ladle to be
   absorbed before the next one goes in.
6. After about eighteen minutes the rice should be tender with a little bite
   left in the middle.
7. Fold the mushrooms back in with the parmesan, take it off the heat, and let
   it sit covered for three minutes before serving.


# Lemon Drizzle Cake

The drizzle is the point. Do not skip the skewering step or it sits on top and
sets like a lid.

- 200g butter, softened
- 200g caster sugar
- 200g self-raising flour
- 4 eggs
- 2 lemons, zest and juice
- 100g icing sugar

1. Heat the oven to 180C and line a loaf tin with baking paper.
2. Beat the butter and caster sugar together until the mixture is pale and
   noticeably lighter.
3. Add the eggs one at a time, beating well between each so the batter does not
   split.
4. Fold in the flour and the lemon zest with a light hand.
5. Scrape into the tin and bake for 45 minutes, until a skewer comes out clean.
6. Mix the lemon juice with the icing sugar to make the drizzle.
7. While the cake is still hot, spike it all over with the skewer and pour the
   drizzle across the top so it sinks in.
8. Leave it in the tin until completely cool.


# Chickpea And Spinach Curry

Twenty minutes, one pan, and it is better the next day.

- 2 tins chickpeas, drained
- 200g spinach
- 1 tin chopped tomatoes
- 1 onion, diced
- 3 cloves garlic, crushed
- 2 teaspoons garam masala
- 1 teaspoon ground cumin
- a thumb of ginger, grated

1. Soften the onion in oil over a medium heat for six or seven minutes.
2. Add the garlic, ginger and spices and cook for another minute, until the
   kitchen smells like the spices rather than the oil.
3. Tip in the tomatoes and let them bubble down for five minutes.
4. Add the chickpeas and simmer for ten minutes, topping up with a splash of
   water if it gets too thick.
5. Stir the spinach through in handfuls, letting each one wilt before the next.
6. Season, and finish with a squeeze of lemon.


# Overnight Oats

The recipe that is mostly waiting.

- 50g rolled oats
- 100ml milk or yoghurt
- 1 tablespoon seeds
- honey to taste

1. Put the oats, milk and seeds in a jar and stir until nothing is dry.
2. Seal it and leave it in the fridge overnight.
3. In the morning, loosen it with a splash more milk and sweeten to taste.


# Roast Chicken Thighs With Potatoes

One tray. The potatoes cook in the fat, which is the entire trick.

- 8 chicken thighs, bone in
- 1kg potatoes, halved
- 1 lemon, quartered
- 4 sprigs rosemary
- olive oil, salt, pepper

1. Heat the oven to 200C.
2. Toss the potatoes with oil, salt and the rosemary in a large roasting tray.
3. Season the chicken thighs well and lay them skin side up on top of the
   potatoes.
4. Tuck the lemon quarters in among everything.
5. Roast for 50 minutes without touching it.
6. Check the thickest thigh is cooked through, then let the tray rest for five
   minutes before serving.


# A Note On Stock

Shop-bought stock is fine and nobody is checking. If you do make your own,
freeze it flat in bags so it stacks, and remember that a stock made from a
roast chicken carcass is the cheapest good thing in cooking.
